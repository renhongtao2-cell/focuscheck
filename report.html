05Talking to a clinician

If your result was at or above the screening threshold, the most useful next step is a conversation with a doctor or psychologist. Many people delay this for years because they are not sure their experience is “serious enough”. Screening results give you a concrete starting point.

What to bring

  • This report, including the appendix of your individual responses.
  • Concrete examples — two or three specific situations where symptoms caused a real problem at work, at home, or in a relationship.
  • Childhood history, if you can recall it. ADHD symptoms typically have roots in childhood, and clinicians will ask. School reports, old comments from teachers, or a parent’s recollection are genuinely useful.
  • Your timeline — when the difficulties started, and whether they have been lifelong or recent. Recent onset points to different causes.

What to expect

  • A clinical interview covering symptoms, history and daily impact.
  • Standardised rating scales — often the same ASRS you just completed, plus others.
  • Questions that rule out other explanations: sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, thyroid problems, and in some cases learning difficulties.
  • Usually more than one appointment. A good assessment is not a five-minute form.

Questions worth asking

  • Do my symptoms meet the criteria, and which presentation fits best?
  • What else could explain these symptoms, and how would we tell the difference?
  • What are the treatment options, and what does the evidence say about each?
  • What should I expect from medication, therapy, or both — and on what timeline?
  • What can I do in the meantime that is likely to help?
Important. A screening result is not a diagnosis, and it cannot rule a condition in or out. Only a qualified professional can do that, and only after a proper evaluation. Use this report to start a conversation — not to end one.
