Adult ADHD symptoms, explained

The WHO Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS-v1.1) contains 18 statements. Here they all are, with what each one is really measuring — plus exactly how the screening score is calculated.

18 statements 2 symptom clusters WHO ASRS-v1.1

Why a checklist and not a list of “signs”

Most symptom lists you will find online are written from the outside — what ADHD looks like to someone watching. That is the wrong vantage point for adults, because most adult symptoms are internal. Nobody can see you lose the thread of a conversation, or feel unable to start a task you genuinely want to finish.

The ASRS is different. It is a validated instrument developed with the World Health Organization, and every item is written in the first person, about your own experience. It is widely used in adult ADHD research and as a first-stage screener in clinical settings.

The two symptom clusters

The 18 items split into two clusters of nine. Which cluster dominates is what determines the presentation — inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, or combined. It does not determine whether ADHD is present.

  • Inattention — 9 items covering focus, follow-through, working memory and organisation.
  • Hyperactivity and impulsivity — 9 items covering restlessness, difficulty unwinding, and the gap between impulse and action.

All 18 statements

Part A (items 1–6) is the validated short screener. Part B (items 7–18) adds detail and is used for the cluster breakdown, not for the threshold score.

Each statement is answered on a five-point frequency scale: Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Often, Very Often.

How the screening score is calculated

Not every answer counts the same way. The six Part A items each have their own threshold for counting as “positive” — and items 1–3 have a lower bar than items 4–6.

Part A itemsCounts as positive at
1, 2, 3Sometimes or above
4, 5, 6Often or above

Four or more positives out of six places you at or above the screening threshold. That is the validated cut-off, and it is the number that matters for deciding whether a professional evaluation is worth pursuing.

The cluster percentages shown alongside it are different: each cluster is scored as a proportion of its maximum possible load across all nine items. They are descriptive rather than diagnostic — useful for seeing which kind of difficulty you reported more of, not for deciding whether ADHD is present.

One known limitation. Only two of the six Part A items (5 and 6) cover hyperactivity and impulsivity. Someone with a strongly hyperactive-impulsive picture can therefore score below the threshold simply because the screener asked about inattention more than it asked about restlessness. If that describes you, the Part B answers in the 12–18 range are worth reading carefully and worth mentioning to a clinician.

What else can look like ADHD

A screener is deliberately tuned to avoid missing possible cases. The trade-off is that it also flags people whose symptoms come from somewhere else. These are common, checkable, and often reversible:

  • Sleep debt and sleep disorders. Chronic short sleep mimics attention problems almost perfectly, including the “driven” feeling from over-tiredness.
  • Anxiety. Racing thoughts and difficulty settling can look like inattention and restlessness at the same time.
  • Depression and low mood. Reduced concentration, low motivation and slowed task initiation overlap heavily with the inattentive cluster.
  • Chronic stress or burnout. Sustained load degrades working memory and follow-through in ways that look identical on a questionnaire.
  • Thyroid problems and some vitamin deficiencies. Both can produce fatigue, poor focus and restlessness — and both are identified with a simple blood test.
  • Perimenopause and hormonal shifts. Commonly associated with a marked change in focus and memory that is not ADHD.

ADHD symptoms, by contrast, typically have roots in childhood and have been present in some form for as long as you can remember. Recent onset points somewhere else.

What to do with a result

  1. Below the threshold? That does not automatically mean nothing is going on. It means your responses do not currently indicate clinically significant ADHD symptoms. If the difficulties are real and persistent, track them for two to three weeks and speak to a professional anyway.
  2. At or above the threshold? The useful next step is a conversation with a doctor or psychologist. Bring a written summary of your responses — it makes the appointment far more productive than trying to describe it from memory.
  3. Either way, rule out the basics first. Sleep, stress, mood and thyroid function are cheap to check and frequently explain the picture on their own.
  4. Write down concrete examples. Two or three specific situations where symptoms caused a real problem at work, at home, or in a relationship are worth more in an assessment than any score.
